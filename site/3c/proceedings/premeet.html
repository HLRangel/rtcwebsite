From the Center

Important Pre-Meeting Observations of the 3rd Yearly Meeting

TBLOC Goals

One of the main future aims of the TBLOC project is archiving and preserving the websites we list. As such, we should figure out a way of doing web crawling without completely hammering our storage space.

My proposal is that we don’t crawl whole websites for now, and instead focus on creating some sort of browser extension that would allow users to capture a page from one of the websites on the list (or submit a suggestion to include a site on the list). I know this is probably putting too much faith on the ability of the average online communist to reliably click a button, but it’s one of the options, and one I’m favorable towards because it creates a small amount of continuous engagement between our project and the outside world.

Obviously, just straight-up running something like HTTrack on a bunch of webpages and distributing the zip files is also an option, but an extension would allow us to introduce some very interesting dynamics. We could regulate the amount of demand for archival services via regulating positive reinforcement, introducing little “daily goals” which we then scale back on when we need to expand storage. The possibilities are endless for such a simple piece of software. Writing browser extensions is annoying, though, so if we go forth with this plan, I recommend we start with a Chrome extension in order to reach the widest possible audience for the least amount of work.

Eventually, once we have enough data, it’d be interesting to focus on a cold storage project. This is something that the Marxists Internet Archive itself used to do, but eventually gave up on because of the sheer amount of things they hosted.

Additional goal: user-side blog engine

It would be wise to develop a program allowing for us to do user-side rendering of Markdown or AsciiDoc text as part of a wider blog engine. Something that requires none of our input at all to publish a text outside of dropping a text file into a directory and changing a JSON file is desirable and can help our future archival efforts if we ever decide to provide transcriptions of scans or something similar.

We first need to work on the WebAssembly tooling for such a program, as writing even simple pieces of software in JavaScript can be complicated. I suggest we use C. We’ll need to write a memory allocator in order to do anything useful, along with reimplementing a few standard library functions. This would require some work, but the upside is that it can be re-used for other projects, including our extension.

The RTC’s education and outreach tasks

Over the last 3 years, I’ve slowly realized that attempts at outreach towards organizations, especially on the matter of operational security, are ineffective. Any real change in the way things go has to come from within, and our experience shows us that it cannot be a prescribed change: forms arise naturally, and militants can only succeed if they’re all endowed with the conscience of the effects of their actions.

This seems almost like a truism, but it hides within it a rejection of bureaucratic forms, which seek to recreate apparatuses from the last century with a hundreth of the required quorum. As systems of social regulation and control become more widespread and decentralized, the role of the “social brain” of the organization, of the procedures that arise from the relationship between militants, becomes more important. There is not any room for uncertain and inefficient prescription, as our movement has not yet achieved the critical mass required to make its purpose arise above any sort of failure in form.

Effectively, this means we need to take people into our work. Even something as simple as what we have, which is an online space for technical exchange and software development, can have a real effect on how future practical positions develop, much like the letter groups of yesteryear had their own influence. Of course, we won’t provoke the revolution by writing software or archiving, instead - and this is a position that all groups agree with to some extent - there is important experience to be gained from being part of this work. Being able to articulate knowledge in a pedagogical manner critical for all education, including education around communist practice.

What we have to ensure in the future are opportunities for participation. The mere assignment of tasks, like what happens in some environments, is futile and discouraging. We also need to ensure our political tasks do not interfere with the distribution of our work, because after all, most software is relatively un-opinionated outside of its specific design. One wouldn’t publish a mathematical formula as a statement in a political journal because this only has a constraining effect and is a waste of effort: essentially, where possible, we must create independent projects that are useful on their own, which we then instrumentalize for our own goals.

Outreach: the political situation of the technical-scientific space

Software development is one of the handful of matters in the exact sciences where there is a strong inter-class non-academic community acting for a purpose other than personal wealth or prestige. Of course, I am referring here to the utopian ideals of the Free/Libre Software movement (not open-source in general), focused around the Free Software Foundation (FSF), which is recently facing the ascension of a bourgeois-progressive grassroots movement.

The FSF is very important overall. It is the main apparatus behind the things that actually make Linux machines useful, such as the C Library and coreutils, along with many other projects. If you want to learn what they do, there’s no better resource than their website. During the late 2000s and early 2010s, they had a real political presence that spilled over to the outside world, and outside the US, were the main people consulted when it came to organizing the petty-bourgeois movement for “technological sovereignty”. Of course, this conjecture doesn’t exist anymore, and they have lost a significant amount of influence over time, but the devotees of libre software are still there, and they are upset.

Over the last few years, one of the main causes of internal strife has been the tenure of board member Richard Stallman, the founder of the FSF and its former president. He can really be considered the utopian thinker of computing. You should read some of his work instead of relying on any half-baked summary we could provide.

Ever since 2019, the FSF has been under pressure to get rid Stallman due to a variety of controversial statements. While a lot of what he said is pretty bad, some of it is also mischaracterized. To learn more, read the open latter calling for his resignation, along with the response to it.

Stallman lost the presidency, but was reinstated to the board of the FSF in 2021, and retained influence even during his suspension. Geoffrey Knauth took his place as interim, however, he has been occupying the role for 5 years.

The big recent change comes from the grouping around Drew DeVault, creator of the Hare programming language and founder of the alternative git hosting service SourceHut. Previously just another software developer, he had a change of heart a few years ago and positioned himself as one of the Free Software community’s main figures against misogyny and other forms of harassment, garnering a fair amount of support where others have mostly failed. This is not only because he’s a white man (though it’s definitely helped), but also due to his charisma and non-conciliatory stance: where previously one would have tried to work within established forms of project management, he circumvents them completely, avoiding the classic trap of endless bureaucratic discussions.

His latest enemy has been the FSF in general, with the distaste for the organization being embodied by the figure of Stallman in particular. One of his biggest tactics has been traffic capture via the “Write Free Software” website, where budding software developers are attracted away from the FSF and into a (far more friendlier and easier to interact with) community of programmers.

It all culminated in October 14th, when “The Stallman report” was released, containing a series of allegations of harassment (including emotional extortion), a general stubborn attitude towards change, along with union-busting within the FSF and behaving like a typical petty-bourgeois tyrant (that’s the movement he represents, after all). Now, there’s a disciplinary hearing scheduled for next week at the organization, where things may or may not change.

The Free Software movement, while it is petty-bourgeois and sometimes even has a reactionary character, requires our observation. It is truly a “mass movement of the trade school”, in that it actually is inter-class, unlike many single-issue platforms. There are, today, many waiters and janitors writing free software, just as there are executives doing so. As such, it represents an organic utopian movement and is the main counterbalance between the wholly profit-seeking nature of much of the discourse around programming. Its nature as a social project above a technical one also tames the wholly reactionary nature of the mediocre disciples of technical excellence.

The replacement of the FSF with a movement more aligned to that of the bourgeois left in general would be a negative development, especially during an era where open-source at large is in crisis. While getting involved in petty squabbles is a waste of time for us, the domino effect of this could be silently consequential to the whole technical space.
